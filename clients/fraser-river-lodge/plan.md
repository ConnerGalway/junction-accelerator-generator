## Goal

Corporate is already the biggest part of the business, and it is the part that is growing. You estimated it at about 45 percent of revenue against 20 percent for weddings, with the rest coming from individual stays and sturgeon fishing. Corporate bookings were up year over year while individual stays softened over the summer. You also told us why corporate is where you want to put the bulk of your energy. A wedding means a different menu and a different turnover every day. A corporate group stays a few days, eats a plated dinner and breakfast, is easier on the housekeepers and the chef, and earns more. The goal of the next ninety days is to make it easier for the right corporate buyer to find Fraser River Lodge, understand what they are getting, and raise their hand earlier.

The top of the funnel is not the problem. The digital assessment scored the lodge 89 out of 100, one of the higher scores we have seen. You have 519 Google reviews at 4.8 stars, 51,477 followers across your main platforms, and an Instagram engagement rate of 20.42 percent, roughly seven to ten times the tourism average. Corporate guests are already finding you through your newsletters, Instagram, trade shows, word of mouth and the Nation's network, and you post at least two pieces of corporate content a week. The gap is in the middle. Right now there is no way to see what happens between someone watching a reel and someone filling in the contact form. The corporate brochure link is not tracked. The corporate page offers a menu of options instead of solving the planner's problem. And an event planner who is six months away from booking has no reason to give you their name today, so you never hear from them until they are ready, if they come back at all.

Two things stand in the way of the buyers who do find you. The first is location. People in Vancouver ask where Agassiz is, even though the address is on everything. The second is price. You described the sticker shock that comes when a buyer compares your quote against a hotel that has only priced rooms and catering, when yours includes the venue, meals, activities and privacy, with no reason to leave the property. Both are explanation problems, and both can be solved with better content.

The order of the work follows from that. Weeks 1 to 4 build the foundation you control: a baseline of where your inquiries come from today, a written brief for the new corporate landing page, and a first look at whether ChatGPT, Claude and Google's AI answers name you for corporate retreat searches. Weeks 5 to 8 put a lead magnet in front of planners and start a corporate YouTube presence built for search. Weeks 9 to 12 bring in outside voices through partner collaborations and a hosted retreat that becomes a case study. Paid media is deliberately not in this plan. Until you can measure which content produces good leads, there is no case for asking your director to spend on it. Step 14 of Tactic 1 builds that case for you at day 90.

There is no timeline for the new website, so this plan does not depend on it. The landing page brief will be ready whenever the redesign starts, and everything else runs on channels you already manage. Be realistic about what the ninety days produce. Corporate buyers often plan months ahead, so most of the bookings this work influences will land after the plan ends. What you should have at day 90 is a finished brief in the web team's hands, a lead magnet collecting the names of planners who are not ready to book yet, every new inquiry tagged with its source, measurably more AI search mentions, a small library of corporate video, and a partner retreat agreed in writing. That is the funnel your director will want to see before any budget follows.

## Strategic Positioning

Most corporate venues within reach of Vancouver sell a room. A hotel near the airport sells a meeting room with catering. A golf resort sells the golf, which is why Sandpiper keeps showing up in searches, and you do not offer golf. Fraser River Lodge sells the whole retreat. A group arrives, meets in a hall that holds up to 200 or splits into breakout sessions, eats on site, sturgeon fishes, throws axes, plays yard games, sleeps on site and leaves without having organised anything else. You put it in four words: "not another conference room."

Two things make that offer hard to copy. The first is the all-in-one model, which you named as what turns sticker shock into a yes. The second is that the lodge is Indigenous owned. You told us you believe corporate guests value an offsite that is also a cultural experience, one that supports a local Nation. That is a reason for a company to choose you that has nothing to do with price, and it should sit near the top of the corporate story, not at the bottom.

What holds the offer back is that it has to be explained, and the current corporate page lists options instead. A planner comparing three quotes needs to see, in one place, everything your price replaces. A planner in Vancouver needs to read that you are about 1.5 hours away and [CONFIRM: 10 or 15 minutes drive] from Harrison Hot Springs before they read the word Agassiz. That second point lines up with work already under way. Tourism Harrison is building awareness of the Harrison River Valley as a region that includes Agassiz, and you have already started using that language in your posts. The more the lodge presents itself as the corporate base of the Harrison River Valley, the easier your location becomes to explain.

> Fraser River Lodge is the all-in-one corporate retreat in the Harrison River Valley, about 1.5 hours from Vancouver. Your team meets, eats, stays and plays in one Indigenous-owned property against the mountains, with nothing else to book and no reason to leave. It is not another conference room.

## Tactic 1: Write the Corporate Landing Page Brief and Launch a Lead Magnet
Phase: Foundation
Short Label: Corporate Page & Lead Magnet
Subtitle: Turn what you know about corporate buyers into a landing page brief for the new site, and give planners who are not ready to book a reason to share their contact details now.

### What We Heard From You

You told us your biggest challenge is turning strong visibility and traffic into qualified leads, particularly for corporate events, and that there is a gap between social and bookings that is difficult to see into. A corporate inquiry currently starts with the contact form, which lands in your inbox or the events inbox. Nobody tracks the "View corporate brochure" link today. You have very little input on the website, and there is a redesign coming but no timeline for it. You described a qualified corporate lead as a group staying three to four days, adding excursions, food and activities, and coming back in future years. You said price is the main reason people do not book, and the all-in-one experience is what changes their mind. You have already written a blog post on where to start planning a corporate retreat. The January giveaway on the wedding page noticeably lifted wedding inquiries, which tells us your audience will trade contact details for something they want.

### What This Is

There are two pieces of work. The first is a written brief that tells whoever builds the new site exactly what the corporate landing page needs to do, section by section, with the form fields and the tracking specified. You can hand it over the day the redesign starts. The second is a lead magnet: a useful resource for corporate planners, offered in exchange for an email address, followed by a short series of emails that hands warm leads to the events team. You test it with small free versions first, so you only build the one planners actually want.

### Why It Matters

Every other tactic in this plan sends corporate buyers somewhere, and right now that somewhere is a page that makes them do the work. The brief means the new site is designed around the buyer from the first wireframe, instead of being fixed after launch. The lead magnet catches the executive assistant planning an event six months out, who would otherwise browse, leave and possibly book elsewhere. It also gives you the first number between social and bookings you have ever been able to report: how many planners raised their hand.

### Implementation Steps

#### Part A: Know the Buyer Before You Brief Anyone

1. **Pull twelve months of corporate inquiries.**
   - Ask the events manager for every corporate inquiry from the past twelve months. A forwarded inbox folder or the tracking sheet you mentioned is fine.
   - Record six things per inquiry in one spreadsheet: date received, company type, group size, number of nights, how they heard about you, and whether it booked.
   - Add a seventh column for the reason when it did not book, even if the answer is a guess. You expect most of these to say price, and the brief needs to know how often that is true.
   - Total the "how they heard" column. This is your baseline. Write the totals at the top of the sheet: [CONFIRM: corporate inquiries in the last 12 months] inquiries and [CONFIRM: corporate bookings in the last 12 months] bookings.

2. **Talk to three recent corporate bookers.**
   - Ask the events manager to pick three: one large group such as the returning university workshop, one small group from out east, and one that nearly did not book.
   - Keep each call to fifteen minutes. Ask: How did you first hear about us? What did you search for before you found us? Who else were you comparing us with? What almost stopped you? What did you have to explain to your boss to get approval?
   - Write down their exact words. "Somewhere the team won't be checking email" is worth more in a headline than anything we could write.
   - If a call is not possible, send the same five questions by email.

3. **Walk the current corporate path as if you were a planner.**
   - On your phone, start from your Instagram bio link and try to get to a corporate inquiry. Screenshot every screen.
   - Count the taps from bio to submitted form. Note every point where you had to choose between options. At the moment, the Corporate Group Retreats page presents four options.
   - Note where the location first appears in terms a Vancouver planner understands, and where the first price or price guide appears.
   - Do the same from a Google search for "corporate retreat near Vancouver". Keep the screenshots for the brief, since they show the web team the problem faster than words can.

#### Part B: Write the Landing Page Brief

4. **Write the page around the planner's problem.**
   - The planner is not asking whether you have a conference room. They want their team to have a great few days, and they do not want to coordinate five vendors to make it happen. Every section should answer that.
   - Specify this order of sections in the brief:
     - **Headline:** an outcome-led headline that names the location in Vancouver terms, for example "Your team's retreat, fully handled. 1.5 hours from Vancouver in the Harrison River Valley."
     - **How it works:** a short section, from first call to departure.
     - **Packages:** three done-for-you retreat packages (see Step 5).
     - **What's included:** the full list.
     - **Sample itinerary:** a two-day example.
     - **Why Indigenous owned matters:** a short section on what that means for the group.
     - **Proof:** past corporate clients (only with permission) and recent reviews that mention retreats or teams.
     - **FAQ.**
     - **Inquiry form.**
   - Ask for one primary call to action repeated down the page ("Plan your retreat") and one secondary call to action for the lead magnet ("Get the planning checklist"). Do not add a third.
   - Ask for the brochure download to stay, but behind the tracked link specified in Step 7.
   - Ask for the page to be written in your words from the calls in Step 2, not in brochure language.

5. **Package the retreat so the price makes sense on first read.**
   - Base three packages on what you already sell. A one-day workshop, like the university group that uses the hall, lunch and breakouts. A two-night team retreat, like the groups of ten from out east. A three- to four-day leadership or company retreat, which is your ideal booking.
   - For each package, list exactly what is included: rooms, meals, meeting space, activities, AV, and coordination by the events team.
   - Add a "what this replaces" line under each package. List the separate things a planner would otherwise have to book themselves, such as a venue, a caterer, accommodation, an activity provider and transport between them. This is your answer to sticker shock, in writing.
   - Give a starting price or per-person range for each package: [CONFIRM: starting price or per-person range for each corporate package]. If the team will not publish prices, ask for "packages from $X" at minimum. A planner who cannot find any price at all often assumes it is out of reach.
   - Agree the package names and inclusions with the events manager before they go into the brief.

6. **Specify a dedicated corporate inquiry form.**
   - Ask for a form on the corporate page that is separate from general reservations and routes straight to the events inbox, with you copied.
   - Fields to specify: name, email, phone, company, role, group size (as ranges: under 15, 15 to 40, 40 to 100, 100 to 200), preferred dates or month, number of nights, and "What would make this retreat a success?"
   - Add a required "How did you hear about us?" dropdown matching the sources in your Step 1 spreadsheet: Instagram, Facebook, YouTube, LinkedIn, Google search, AI assistant such as ChatGPT, newsletter, trade show, word of mouth, Nation network, other.
   - Ask for a confirmation message that sets expectations, for example that the events team replies within a few hours on business days. You already follow up that fast, so say so.

7. **Specify the tracking the new page must include.**
   - List these as required, not optional, so they are in the build quote from the start. Each of the following should be recorded as a GA4 event:
     - Corporate inquiry form submitted
     - Brochure downloaded
     - Lead magnet sign-up
     - Phone number tapped
   - Ask for the source of each submission (the UTM parameters from Step 11 and Tactic 2) to be captured in a hidden form field. Then the events inbox shows where every lead came from, without anyone checking analytics.
   - Ask for the phone number to be a tap-to-call link everywhere on the page. The assessment found it is not clickable on the current site.
   - Find out who has access to the current GA4 property and Meta Pixel: [CONFIRM: who owns GA4 and Meta Pixel access; likely the SEO and ads specialist]. Ask them to review this section of the brief before it goes to the web team.

8. **Take the brief to Courtney and agree what can change before the new site exists.**
   - Bring the finished brief, the Step 3 screenshots and the Step 1 totals to a thirty-minute meeting with Courtney.
   - Ask for two decisions. First, that the brief becomes the agreed specification for the corporate section of the redesign. Second, which small changes can happen on the current site in the meantime.
   - Suggested interim changes are all small: make the phone number tap-to-call, add "1.5 hours from Vancouver" to the corporate page's first paragraph, swap the brochure link for a tracked link, and add a link to the lead magnet once it exists.
   - Save the brief somewhere the web team will find it when the redesign starts, and note the date it was approved.

#### Part C: Build the Lead Magnet

9. **Pick two lead magnets from the menu.**
   - Planners will give you their email for something that makes their job easier or makes them look good to their boss. Here are six options that fit what you sell:
     - **The Corporate Retreat Planning Checklist and Timeline.** A six-month countdown from "pick a date" to "send the agenda." You already know there is demand, because you wrote the blog post on where to start.
     - **The Retreat Approval Kit.** A short document a planner or executive assistant can forward to their leadership. It covers what is included, a sample agenda, a per-person cost range, travel time from Vancouver, and why the cultural element matters. It answers the question they will be asked before they are allowed to book.
     - **The All-In Cost Worksheet.** A simple sheet that adds up what a piecemeal retreat costs (venue, caterer, rooms, activities, transport) next to an all-in-one package. It works as a lead magnet on its own, or as a page inside the Approval Kit.
     - **Sample agendas.** Ready-to-use agendas for a one-day workshop, a two-night team retreat and a three-day leadership offsite.
     - **The Team Activity Guide.** Sturgeon fishing, axe throwing, yard games and [CONFIRM: cultural experiences available to corporate groups], with timings and group sizes for each.
     - **The Open Dates List.** Shoulder-season dates still available, updated quarterly. This one is strongest with buyers who are ready to book and weakest at catching early planners.
   - Test the Planning Checklist and the Retreat Approval Kit first. The checklist matches demand you have already seen. The Approval Kit tackles sticker shock directly, and it is built for the executive assistant who has to justify the venue upward.

10. **Test demand with small versions before building anything big.**
    - For three weeks, publish a small free version of each. For the checklist, a carousel of the first five steps. For the Approval Kit, a carousel or LinkedIn post on "how to get your retreat approved."
    - Post each on Instagram, Facebook and LinkedIn, inside your existing two corporate posts a week rather than in addition to them.
    - Track saves, shares, comments, DMs and link clicks for each in one row of a sheet. On Instagram, saves are the best single signal of usefulness.
    - End each post with a soft call to action: "Comment CHECKLIST and we'll send you the full version when it's ready." This also builds the list of people to email on launch day.
    - Pick the winner at the end of Week 6. If they are close, build the checklist first, since it is quicker to make.

11. **Build the winner and host it without waiting for the new site.**
    - Make the full version as a branded PDF of eight to twelve pages, in whatever design tool the team uses for the corporate brochure.
    - Host the sign-up on your email platform's own form or landing page feature, so you do not need web team time: [CONFIRM: email marketing platform]. Once the platform is confirmed, we can give you the exact menu path.
    - Use five fields only: first name, email, company, role, and approximate group size. Every extra field costs sign-ups.
    - Tag everyone who signs up as "Corporate: [lead magnet name]" so they never get your general traveller newsletter by accident.
    - Build every link to the form with Google's Campaign URL Builder, using a different utm_source for each place it appears (instagram_bio, linkedin, youtube, newsletter). That way you can see which channel produces sign-ups.
    - Put the link in your Instagram bio link-in-bio as the first corporate link, in the Facebook page's About section, in the YouTube descriptions from Tactic 2, and in a new paragraph at the end of the existing corporate retreat blog post.

12. **Write the follow-up emails.**
    - Write four short emails that go out automatically after sign-up:
      - **Email 1, immediately:** the download link, and one line on who you are.
      - **Email 2, day 3:** how a booking works, from first call to departure, linking to the walkthrough video from Tactic 2.
      - **Email 3, day 7:** one real story of a corporate group's retreat. Use the partner case study from Tactic 3 once it exists.
      - **Email 4, day 14:** a direct invitation to talk to the events manager, using their name, with a reply-to that goes to them.
    - Keep each email under 150 words, with one link.
    - After Email 4, move sign-ups into a corporate segment of your newsletter. You said newsletters are already one of your strongest corporate channels.

#### Part D: Close the Loop With Sales

13. **Set up a lead-quality loop with the events team.**
    - Add four columns to the Step 1 spreadsheet: lead magnet signed up for (yes or no), date of first conversation, proposal sent, and booked value.
    - Meet the events manager for twenty minutes once a month. Ask one question about the month's leads: "Are these the people you want more of?"
    - If sign-ups are high but conversations are low, the lead magnet is attracting the wrong people. Adjust the role and group size fields before promoting it further.

14. **Build the budget case for your director at day 90.**
    - You said you would need to know something works before you ask for budget. This step gives you that evidence.
    - Write one page with:
      - Sign-ups by source.
      - The number that became conversations and proposals.
      - Any bookings and their value.
      - The events manager's quality rating.
    - Ask for a specific test: a fixed amount for six weeks, behind the lead magnet only, with a target cost per sign-up. Leave the amount to the media specialist and your director: [CONFIRM: available marketing budget].
    - If the results are thin, say so, and ask for another ninety days of organic testing instead. A credible "not yet" protects your next request.

### Tips

- Write the brief in plain language, not web jargon. "When someone submits this form, the events inbox should see which link they came from" is a clearer instruction than any technical term, and it is harder to misread.
- Use your guests' words in the headline. If two of the three people in Step 2 say the same phrase, that phrase belongs at the top of the page.
- Do not let the lead magnet turn into a second brochure. A checklist that helps a planner even if they never book with you is what earns the email address. The case for the lodge belongs in Emails 2 to 4.
- Give the events manager a preview of the lead magnet before launch, so they recognise it when someone mentions it on a call.
- Keep the January wedding giveaway in mind when the team asks whether people will really sign up. Your audience has already shown it will.

## Tactic 2: Get Found in Search and AI Answers for Corporate Retreats
Phase: Momentum
Short Label: Search & AI Content
Subtitle: Shape what search engines, AI tools and YouTube read about the lodge, so a planner asking where to take their team finds Fraser River Lodge and the reason to choose it.

### What We Heard From You

You told us the lodge was not showing up in ChatGPT for a long time, and that restarting the blog was partly a response to that. You have already been trying question-and-answer captions, such as asking "where can I find a cozy lodge in the Fraser Valley?" and answering from the lodge's point of view, without knowing yet whether they are working. You want the lodge to show up as one of the top choices more often, on par with competitors like Sandpiper. You already send a post-stay review email to every guest. When we suggested shaping it so corporate guests mention their retreat, you said you would take it back and implement it right away. On YouTube, you said the channel converts well but currently only covers fishing and water. You suggested a mock corporate retreat and a walkthrough of how easy it is to book as a corporate group. When we raised the risk of straying off-topic on the existing channel, you said starting a new channel is no big deal.

### What This Is

This tactic covers everything you publish or control that search engines and AI tools read when a planner asks where to take their team. That includes your listings and reviews, LinkedIn and the blog, and a new corporate YouTube channel. It all runs from one shared list of planner questions and is measured by one monthly check. AI assistants look for the most consistent answer across many sources, so the aim is for the phrase "corporate retreat" to be attached to Fraser River Lodge everywhere the lodge appears.

### Why It Matters

This is the part of search you can influence without touching the website. Planners increasingly start with a question like "best corporate retreat venues near Vancouver", and the AI answer names three or four places. Google has replaced the Questions and Answers section of Business Profiles with an AI "Ask" feature in Maps that builds its answers from your reviews, photos and profile details. YouTube and LinkedIn are among the sources AI tools cite most often. A video titled with the right phrase can keep earning views from planners for years, and it also feeds the AI answers. Your 519 reviews and 1.37 million YouTube views are large assets, but only if enough of that material talks about teams and retreats.

### Implementation Steps

#### Part A: Measure Where You Stand

1. **Build one list of the questions planners actually ask.**
   - This list drives everything in this tactic: the AI checks, the blog titles, the LinkedIn posts and the YouTube titles. Keep it in one sheet so the work stays joined up.
   - In a private browser window, start typing into the YouTube search bar and then into Google: "corporate retreat", "team building BC", "offsite venue", "company retreat ideas", "retreat near Vancouver". Write down every autocomplete suggestion.
   - In Google, also note the "People also ask" questions for the same terms.
   - Add the questions from your Tactic 1 inquiry log and, once the buyer calls in Tactic 1, Step 2 are done, the exact questions those bookers asked before they found you.
   - Aim for at least 25 questions by the end of Week 2, and keep adding to the list as you go.

2. **Run a baseline AI search check.**
   - Choose twelve questions from the Step 1 list that a corporate planner would type into an AI tool. For example:
     - "best corporate retreat venues near Vancouver"
     - "team offsite lodge in the Fraser Valley with accommodation for 30"
     - "Indigenous-owned corporate retreat in BC"
     - "corporate retreat near Harrison Hot Springs"
     - "all-inclusive team retreat in the BC mountains"
     - "venue for a 150-person company event with overnight rooms near Vancouver"
   - Ask each question in ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and Google, recording Google's AI answer at the top of the results where one appears. Use a private browser window so your own history does not colour the answers.
   - Record the results in a sheet: question, tool, whether Fraser River Lodge is named, whether Sandpiper is named, which other venues appear, and how the lodge is described.
   - That gives you 48 checks. Your baseline is the number of checks that name the lodge. Save the date and the sheet. Each month you repeat the exact same questions.

3. **List the sources the AI tools cite.**
   - Perplexity, and ChatGPT when it searches the web, show the links they drew on. Copy every source cited for the corporate questions into a second tab of the sheet.
   - Group the sources: review sites, directories and venue listing sites, tourism organisations, news and media, blogs by planners or vendors, YouTube, LinkedIn.
   - Mark which ones already mention Fraser River Lodge. The ones that mention competitors but not you are the target list for Tactic 3, Steps 3 and 4.

4. **Re-run the checks every month.**
   - **AI answers:** on the same day each month, repeat the exact Step 2 questions in the same tools and the same private window. Change nothing, so the comparison is fair.
   - **How the lodge is described:** note any change, as well as whether it is named. "A lodge in Agassiz" moving to "an all-inclusive corporate retreat in the Harrison River Valley" is real progress, even before the count moves.
   - **YouTube search terms:** once the corporate channel from Part D has videos, open YouTube Studio, then Analytics, then the Content tab, then the YouTube search traffic source. This shows the exact phrases people searched before they found each video. Add any phrase you had not thought of to the Step 1 list. It is probably your next video or blog title.
   - **Clicks:** record clicks from YouTube, LinkedIn and the blog to the lead magnet and inquiry links, using the tagged links in GA4 or your email platform.
   - Keep all of it on one monthly row. Expect slow movement in month one, because new mentions take time to be picked up. Do not judge the YouTube channel on subscribers. Ten views from the right planners matter more than a thousand from the wrong audience.

#### Part B: Fix the Listings and Reviews AI Tools Read

5. **Claim Bing Places by importing your Google profile.**
   - Go to bingplaces.com and sign in, then choose the option to import from Google Business Profile. A verified Google profile carries its verification across, which saves the postcard step.
   - Check the imported category, address (7984 McDonald Rd S, Agassiz), phone (604-796-1210), website and photos.
   - Bing powers Microsoft Copilot, and research suggests Bing Places listings influence the web results ChatGPT draws on. This takes about twenty minutes and is often missed.

6. **Bring TripAdvisor and Facebook in line with the corporate story.**
   - Your TripAdvisor listing already drives bookings. Check that its description mentions corporate retreats, the 200-person hall and breakout space, and the drive time from Vancouver.
   - Make sure the TripAdvisor photo gallery includes the hall set up for a meeting, not only rooms and scenery.
   - On the Facebook page, update the About section so the first line mentions corporate retreats as well as stays, weddings and fishing.
   - TripAdvisor and Facebook are among the review sources most often pulled into Bing and AI results, so they carry more weight than their traffic suggests.

7. **Make your Google Business Profile describe the corporate offer.**
   - In your Business Profile, open Edit profile and check the hotel details: check-in and check-out times, and the amenities list (meeting space, parking, wifi, accessibility).
   - Add at least ten corporate photos with descriptive file names before upload: the hall set for a meeting, a breakout session, a team at dinner, a team on the river, axe throwing.
   - Publish one Google Post a fortnight with a corporate angle, such as a retreat package, a team activity or a new video. Hotels cannot publish "offer" posts on their profile, so use the standard update type.
   - Ask Maps answers questions like "is this good for groups?" from what is on your profile and in your reviews. The more often the profile shows groups and meetings, the more likely the answer is yes.

8. **Rewrite the post-stay review request for corporate guests.**
   - Ask whoever sends the post-stay email to create a separate version for corporate groups. Send it to the organiser and, where the organiser agrees, to attendees.
   - Change the ask from "leave us a review" to "We would love to hear how your team retreat went. Would you take a minute to share it on Google?" Naming the occasion makes people more likely to describe it.
   - Add two short excerpts from real existing reviews that mention a team, retreat or company event, under the heading "What other teams have said." Use real reviews only, word for word, with the reviewer's first name.
   - Link to both Google and TripAdvisor, with Google first.
   - Do not offer anything in return for a review, and do not tell guests what to write. Google's policies prohibit both. Naming the occasion and showing examples is as far as it should go.
   - Count how many of your current reviews mention corporate, team or retreat, and write that number down: [CONFIRM: count of existing Google reviews mentioning corporate, team or retreat]. That is your baseline.

9. **Reply to reviews using the words you want repeated.**
   - When a corporate guest reviews you, reply within 48 hours and use the natural phrase: "Thank you for bringing your team to Fraser River Lodge for your retreat."
   - Keep replies personal and specific. A reply that repeats keywords without saying anything real reads as spam to guests and to Google.
   - Prioritise the six one-star reviews the assessment found, if any are still unanswered. Acknowledge the concern and offer a direct contact.

#### Part C: Publish Answers on LinkedIn and the Blog

10. **Set up LinkedIn as your corporate channel.**
    - LinkedIn is one of the sources AI tools cite most often for business questions, and your buyers are on it. Check whether a Fraser River Lodge company page exists and who holds admin access: [CONFIRM: LinkedIn company page status and admin].

### Tips

- Treat the monthly check like a scientific test. Same questions, same tools, same private window. If you change the questions, you lose the comparison.
- Do not chase volume with near-identical posts or videos. The spammy version of this work, dozens of thin videos saying "we are the best", may work briefly, but it puts a 4.8-star reputation at risk.
- One question, four outputs. When you write a blog post, film the matching answer video and cut the LinkedIn post in the same week. Each piece takes less time when you are already thinking about the topic.
- Say the location out loud in every video. "We're about an hour and a half from Vancouver, in the Harrison River Valley" spoken on camera ends up in the captions, and the captions are what search reads.
- Good audio matters more than good video. A clip-on microphone for the events manager is the single best upgrade for these videos.
- Keep using question-and-answer captions on Instagram. There is less evidence that Instagram feeds AI answers than LinkedIn, YouTube and the blog do, but people use Instagram as a search engine in its own right.
- Do not cross-post the corporate videos to the fishing channel. The point of the separate channel is to keep each one clear about its subject.

## Tactic 3: Get Partners, Directories and Tourism Harrison Talking About Your Corporate Retreats
Phase: Rhythm
Short Label: Partners & Mentions
Subtitle: Turn your existing collaborations, directory listings and the Tourism Harrison relationship into outside mentions that sell corporate retreats, and host one partner retreat that becomes a case study.

### What We Heard From You

You already collaborate a lot. When a vendor creates content on the property, you co-post on Instagram to reach both audiences. You do influencer collaborations, and photography collaborations where photographers stay over in exchange for the use of their photos. You have not yet done a dedicated collaboration with a vendor, and nothing so far has been built around corporate. When we described the Oak Bay Beach Hotel and Jillian Harris example, a shoulder-season hosted retreat documented as a case study for corporate sales, you said something like that would work really well for the lodge. You also said you would work with your manager on ideas to bring to Tourism Harrison, and that Courtney is the one who deals with Sonia directly.

### What This Is

This tactic covers what other people say about the lodge. It has three parts. Every collaboration you already run now has to produce something on the web that links back, not only an Instagram post. Directories, listing sites and Tourism Harrison get a clear corporate story to publish. And you host one larger partnership: a corporate-style retreat for a partner with an audience of planners or business leaders, in a quiet period, documented start to finish. The hosted retreat becomes a case study, a video, sales material and a source of credible outside mentions.

### Why It Matters

Instagram collaborations reach people, but they leave nothing behind that search engines or AI tools can find. A planner's venue roundup, a directory listing or a Tourism Harrison page that names the lodge as a corporate retreat venue keeps working for years, and it is exactly the kind of independent source AI answers draw on. The hosted retreat solves a different problem. Corporate buyers want proof that a group like theirs had a great time, and right now that proof lives in the events manager's memory rather than in anything you can send.

### Implementation Steps

#### Part A: Upgrade the Collaborations You Already Do

1. **Add a web mention to every collaboration agreement.**
   - Write a one-page collaboration agreement for all future creators, photographers and vendors. If you already use one, add to it.
   - Add one required deliverable: a post on their own website or blog naming Fraser River Lodge, with a link to the lodge. Suggest the phrase you want used, such as "corporate retreat venue in the Harrison River Valley" or "wedding venue near Harrison Hot Springs", depending on the shoot.
   - Agree usage rights in writing. You can use their photos and video in paid advertising, on the website and in sales materials, with credit.
   - Do not include a Google or TripAdvisor review as a deliverable for a comped stay. Both platforms prohibit reviews given in exchange for something.

2. **Go back to the partners you already have.**
   - List every photographer, vendor and creator you have collaborated with in the past twelve months.
   - Check which already mention the lodge on their website. For those who do not, send a short personal note asking whether they would add a portfolio post or venue page, and offer the photo selection or a short write-up to make it easy.
   - Start with anyone who has worked a corporate event. Ask the events manager for regular partners, such as AV, florists, activity providers and caterers, if any are external.
   - Start a running list of every mention that goes live, with the URL and the date. Steps 3 and 4 add to the same list.

#### Part B: Earn Mentions From Directories and Tourism Harrison

3. **Fill the gaps on your mentions target list.**
   - Open the target list from Tactic 2, Step 3. Pick the five sources that mention competitors but not you. Typical examples are corporate venue directories, meeting and incentive listing sites, BC Indigenous tourism listings, business associations, and planner or vendor blogs.
   - For directories and listing sites, submit or update the lodge's listing with the same corporate description you used on LinkedIn: capacity, the all-in-one model, Indigenous ownership, the Harrison River Valley and the drive time from Vancouver.
   - For blogs and roundups written by people, use the approach in Step 2: a short personal note, the photos and a two-sentence description to make it easy.
   - Add every mention that goes live to the running list from Step 2.

4. **Send Tourism Harrison one corporate request.**
   - Tourism Harrison is building out the Harrison River Valley story with content creators and photo shoots, and part of Sonia's role is to advocate for operators like you. Courtney already works with Sonia, so send this through her.
   - Write a one-page wish list with two asks. First, a "corporate retreat in the Harrison River Valley" story or itinerary, with the lodge as the base and other valley experiences as day activities. Second, that at least one of their creator shoots or briefs includes the lodge as a corporate retreat base.
   - Ask for the story to be published as a web page or blog post that links to the lodge, not only as social posts. A page on a regional tourism site is exactly the kind of source AI tools trust.
   - Offer to host the creator or photographer, to make the events team available for interviews, and to supply footage from your corporate YouTube channel.
   - Follow up after four weeks if you have not heard back, and add any page that goes live to the running list from Step 2.

#### Part C: Host One Partner Retreat and Turn It Into a Case Study

5. **Decide what you can offer a partner.**
   - Agree the dates with the events manager. Choose your quietest weeks, when rooms and the hall would otherwise sit empty: [CONFIRM: shoulder-season dates suitable for a hosted retreat].
   - Agree the maximum value you can host (rooms, meals, hall, activities) with Courtney: [CONFIRM: value of hosted package approved]. A quiet-week hosted retreat costs less than its rack rate suggests, but put a cap on it.
   - If the timing works, consider the Oak Bay model of tying the retreat to a charity or community cause. It gives the partner a reason to say yes, and gives the story something beyond marketing.

6. **Choose the partner.**
   - You want someone whose audience includes the people who book corporate retreats. Four options, in order of fit:
     - A BC event planner or destination management company that plans corporate offsites. They bring credibility with planners and may book you again.
     - A business creator with a LinkedIn or podcast audience of founders, managers or HR leaders.
     - A returning corporate client willing to be documented, with their permission and approval of all content.
     - A non-profit or community organisation's leadership retreat, tied to a cause.
   - Check each candidate for three things: an audience you want, an active website or LinkedIn presence, and a history of documenting their events well.
   - Pick a first choice and a backup before you pitch either.

7. **Pitch with clear terms.**
   - Send a short email offering the hosted retreat. Name the dates, what is included and the cap.
   - Ask for these in return:
     - A written piece on their website or LinkedIn about running their retreat at the lodge, with a link.
     - At least one video, or access to raw footage for your YouTube channel.
     - A quote you can use in the case study.
     - A short interview on camera.
   - Agree everything in a simple written agreement using the template from Step 1, including approval rights if the partner is a corporate client.
   - Confirm the arrangement at least six weeks before the dates, so there is time to plan the content capture.

8. **Plan the content capture.**
   - Write a shot list covering arrival, the first session in the hall, breakouts, meals, each activity, any cultural experience, downtime, and departure.
   - Plan three short interviews: the partner on why they chose the lodge, a participant on what surprised them, and the events manager on what it took to put together.
   - Arrange a photographer through an existing collaboration if you can, using the new agreement from Step 1.
   - Plan to capture footage for at least three future YouTube videos in the same visit.

9. **Turn the retreat into a case study.**
   - If the retreat happens inside the ninety days, build the case study straight away. If it is scheduled later, build the template now so it is ready.
   - Use one structure for every format: the group and what they needed, why they chose the lodge, what the two or three days looked like, what they said afterwards, and what it included.
   - Produce it in five forms:
     - A section on the corporate landing page (add it to the Tactic 1 brief).
     - A two-page PDF for the events team to attach to proposals.
     - A video for the corporate YouTube channel in Tactic 2.
     - A LinkedIn post.
     - Email 3 of the lead magnet sequence in Tactic 1.

#### Part D: Build a Network That Recommends You

10. **Build a preferred planner group.**
    - Make a list of eight to ten Vancouver and Fraser Valley corporate planners and executive assistants. Start from past inquiries, trade show contacts, and partners from Step 2.
    - Offer them early access to shoulder-season dates and a familiarisation visit (a half-day tour, lunch and an activity) in exchange for considering the lodge in their next venue shortlist and roundup.
    - Ask any who write venue roundups or newsletters to include the lodge, with a link.
    - Add each planner to the corporate email segment from Tactic 1, so they hear about new packages first.

|---|---|---|---|
| Collaboration agreement with web mention | Template written | Used in every new collaboration | Used in 100 percent of collaborations |

### Tips

- Make it easy for partners to say yes to a web mention. Send them three photos and a two-sentence description they can adapt. Most people say yes when the work is done for them.
- Give Sonia a specific ask, not a general offer to help. A one-page wish list with a story idea, dates you can host and the footage you can supply is far easier for her to act on.
- Do not let the hosted retreat drift into a general influencer stay. If the partner's audience is mostly travellers, it belongs in a different plan.
- Get written permission before naming any past corporate client publicly. Notable names sell, but only if the client is comfortable being named.
- Keep a copy of every signed agreement in one folder. When a partner's photos turn up in an ad two years from now, you will want to prove you had the rights.

## GBP Quick Wins

These are small jobs, most under an hour, that make the lodge easier to find and choose as a corporate venue. None of them depend on the new website or on anything else in the plan.

### On the Google Business Profile

- **Set the check-in and check-out times.** The assessment found incomplete hours on the website. On a lodging profile, Google shows check-in and check-out times rather than standard opening hours, so make sure these are filled in under Edit profile.
- **Add your social links.** In the profile's contact details, add Instagram, Facebook, YouTube (both channels once the corporate one exists) and LinkedIn. AI tools and Google use these links to confirm which accounts belong to you.
- **Check your categories.** Confirm the primary category is the right lodging type, and check whether secondary categories that describe your event business, such as wedding venue or event venue, are available and accurate.
- **Reply to every unanswered review.** The assessment estimated a response rate of about 60 percent. Bring it close to 100 percent, using the corporate phrasing from Tactic 2 where it fits naturally.
